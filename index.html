Emotional foundations

Read the signal
before the behaviour.

Clear concepts help nurses observe with curiosity. A child’s response may carry bodily, emotional, social, and developmental meaning at the same time.

01

Emotion

A rapid whole-person response.

Emotion is an immediate state linked to something important. It can involve body changes, thoughts, facial expression, voice, and an urge to act.

02

Feeling

The personal awareness of emotion.

A feeling is how a person consciously experiences and gives meaning to an emotion. Children need time, language, play, or pictures to share that meaning.

03

Mood

A longer emotional background.

Mood is usually more diffuse and longer-lasting than a single emotion. Notice duration, change from usual behaviour, and its effect on sleep, play, school, or relationships.

Four connected parts

One emotion can be seen
in many places.

Looking at more than one sign prevents quick labels and supports a fuller nursing assessment.

01

Body

Heart rate, tears, tension, energy, sleep, appetite, and stress signals.

02

Meaning

Thoughts, memories, expectations, language, and interpretation of an event.

03

Expression

Face, voice, posture, play, silence, words, approach, avoidance, or protest.

04

Action

An urge to seek comfort, explore, protect oneself, set a boundary, repair, or connect.

CARE REMINDER

One behaviour is never the whole story. Consider physical needs, triggers, developmental capacity, family context, culture, communication ability, and what helps the child feel safe.

Infancy: feelings begin in the body.

Infants express basic emotions before they can use words. A responsive caregiver helps the baby move from distress toward calm — the foundation of regulation and secure attachment.

Emotional regulation

From borrowed calm
to self-management.

Regulation does not mean never feeling upset. It means noticing a feeling, understanding its message, and choosing a response that is safe and useful.

01

Notice

Look for body cues, feeling words, intensity, triggers, and the effect of the environment.

02

Connect

Offer a calm adult, validation, a safe relationship, and basic care before expecting problem-solving.

03

Choose

Use age-appropriate strategies: comfort, breathing, movement, play, a pause, help-seeking, or a next step.

✦

Strategies grow with age.

Infants need co-regulation. Young children benefit from feeling words, play, movement, sensory comfort, and small choices. School-age children can practise routines, support-seeking, and problem-solving. Adolescents can add reflection, privacy, planning, and trusted support.

⌁

Stress can shrink coping space.

Pain, fever, hunger, sleep loss, sensory overload, illness, trauma reminders, family conflict, loss, or unfamiliar care can make regulation harder. Check these needs before expecting a child to use their best coping skills.

RESILIENCE

Resilience does not mean a child is untouched by stress. It grows when children have supportive relationships, predictable routines, chances to practise coping, rest, safety, and adults who notice both what is difficult and what is already helping.

Nursing assessment

Assess the whole
signal.

Emotional support begins with careful observation. The same response can mean fear, pain, fatigue, confusion, loss of control, or a need for connection.

01

Look

Notice face, voice, posture, play, activity, crying, silence, withdrawal, body complaints, and any change from the child’s usual pattern.

OBSERVABLE CUES
02

Ask

Explore what happened before the response. Ask about pain, illness, separation, uncertainty, sensory load, conflict, loss, and what the child understands.

CONTEXT & MEANING
03

Adapt

Match language and support to developmental level. Use play, pictures, simple words, caregiver involvement, and a small safe choice when helpful.

THERAPEUTIC ACTION

Helpful language

“I can see this is hard.”

  • “Tell me what you understand so far.”
  • “You can stay close to your parent while I explain.”
  • “Would you like to choose which step we do first?”

Language to avoid

Do not shame or dismiss.

  • “There is nothing to cry about.”
  • “Big children do not get scared.”
  • “This will not hurt” when discomfort is possible.

Useful lenses

Four ideas to hold
while you observe.

Theories are guides, not labels. Use them to understand the child in context — family, culture, health, and lived experience.

ATTACHMENT THEORY · BOWLBY & AINSWORTH

A secure base makes exploration possible.

When a caregiver responds sensitively and predictably, a child learns that distress can be met with comfort. This does not remove every difficult emotion; it builds confidence that feelings can be survived and shared.

IN PRACTICE

Invite a familiar caregiver into care, use consistent routines, and respond calmly to distress.
