A lifespan perspective

Growth continues.
Context matters.

Emotional development does not stop in childhood. People keep adapting to relationships, roles, health, culture, opportunities, loss, and new sources of meaning.

01

Continuous

There is no final age at which emotional learning ends. People keep developing ways to understand emotion, seek support, communicate needs, and cope with change.

02

Contextual

Health, body changes, relationships, culture, work, family roles, social conditions, and life events all influence how feelings are experienced and expressed.

03

Changeable

Supportive relationships, new skills, meaningful roles, and respectful health care can strengthen coping at any age—even after stress, illness, or loss.

USE AGE TO GUIDE QUESTIONS — NOT TO MAKE ASSUMPTIONS

Ask what this experience means to the person now: what has changed, what support is present, what strengths remain, and what would make care feel safer or more manageable.

Early years: connection becomes a blueprint.

Responsive relationships help young children recognise emotions, borrow calm from adults, and begin to name what they feel. Secure attachment supports exploration and resilience.

Use predictable routines, a calm voice, play, and caregiver involvement to create emotional safety.

Ages are guides, not deadlines. Health, relationships, culture, opportunity, and life events all shape an individual emotional pathway.

A closer look at adulthood

More than one
adult chapter.

Adult emotional development includes growth, responsibility, and change. Roles can bring purpose and connection, but they can also create pressure, loss of control, or a need to adapt.

18–40

Young adulthood

Intimacy, friendships, partnership, education, employment, independence, parenthood, identity, and health decisions can shape emotional wellbeing. Respect privacy, agency, and the person’s own goals.

CARE QUESTIONHow is this affecting your relationships, work, study, rest, or plans?
40–65

Middle adulthood

Adults may balance work, parenting, caregiving, chronic health management, finances, changing relationships, and reflection on purpose. Contribution and support can be powerful strengths.

CARE QUESTIONWhat roles feel most demanding, and what support would make the plan realistic?
AT EVERY AGE

Identity remains central.

Illness or transition can affect self-image and daily roles. Ask about preferred routines, meaningful relationships, values, family responsibilities, work, interests, and what the person wants to preserve.

What shapes the pathway?

Emotion is never
developed alone.

Across life, feelings are influenced by the interaction of biology, relationships, experiences, culture, and the resources available to a person.

01⌁

Brain & body

Temperament, maturation, sleep, pain, illness, hormones, and sensory needs affect how emotions are felt and expressed.

02◌

Relationships

Caregivers, friends, partners, families, and communities model coping and give people a place to share emotion safely.

03✦

Experience & culture

Success, trauma, transition, values, culture, and social conditions all influence how people understand and manage feelings.

FAMILY & CHOSEN SUPPORTS

Illness can shift roles and place pressure on caregivers. Invite the support people the person wants involved while keeping consent, confidentiality, and the person’s own voice central.

CULTURE & COMMUNICATION

There is no single “right” way to show fear, grief, anger, or respect. Ask about preferred language, decision-making, beliefs, practices, and what support feels helpful.

EQUITY IN CARE

Consider practical barriers such as cost, transport, literacy, stigma, disability access, caregiving, safety, and time when discussing coping or planning support.

Health, illness, and change

Every transition carries
emotional meaning.

Diagnosis, pain, hospitalisation, altered routines, caregiving, loss, and changes in independence can affect emotional wellbeing at any stage of life.

↗

Expected transitions

Starting school, puberty, leaving home, employment, partnership, parenthood, caregiving, retirement, and bereavement can bring both hope and uncertainty. Mixed feelings are often understandable.

⌁

Unexpected changes

Accident, diagnosis, disability, job loss, separation, violence, migration under stress, or sudden bereavement can disrupt routines and coping. The meaning depends on history, resources, and support.

✦

Health-care moments

Fear, anger, sadness, guilt, relief, uncertainty, hope, or mixed emotions may appear during care. Notice intensity, duration, safety, daily functioning, and what the person says they need.

WHOLE-PERSON CHECK

Body, mind, and world.

BODY Pain, breathlessness, fatigue, sleep change, sensory needs, medication effects, and function can affect emotion.

MIND Ask what the person understands, fears, hopes for, or feels unable to manage.

WORLD Explore family, roles, finances, culture, beliefs, stigma, safety, and sources of support.

Do not pathologise every difficult feeling. Sadness after loss or fear before a procedure may be understandable. Nursing care offers safety, symptom relief, information, respect, coping support, and referral when distress becomes severe, persistent, risky, or disabling.

Nursing across the lifespan

Ask, observe,
adapt care.

The same feeling may look different at different ages. Select a patient group to see a therapeutic communication focus.

CHILD · THERAPEUTIC STARTER◕

“Show me what feels worrying.”

Children often communicate emotion through play, behaviour, body complaints, or a caregiver. Simple words, choices, visual preparation, and play reduce uncertainty.

NOTICE

Clinginess, silence, regression, restlessness, refusal, or changes in play.

SUPPORT

Involve a trusted adult, keep language concrete, and offer a small safe choice.
